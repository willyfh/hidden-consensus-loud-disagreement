\begin{abstract}
When AI agents, standing in for the researcher, are asked to answer the same research
question from the same data, do they agree on what they find? We test this for machine
learning specifically, where results are directly checkable by execution. Across 240
independent, memory-isolated agent replicates analyzing the UCI Adult/Census Income
dataset under six research questions, we measure \emph{measure-choice diversity}: how many
distinct ways replicates choose to measure the same answer. This turns out to depend
heavily on the specific question, not simply on whether it is worded abstractly or
concretely. One abstract question alone produced 10 distinct model-comparison choices out
of 20 replicates; two of the other three abstract questions, by contrast, converge tightly
on a single measure once cosmetic wording differences are collapsed. Pooled across
questions, abstract framing still roughly doubles diversity over concrete framing (2.6 vs.\
1.3 distinct measures per 10 replicates), but the effect is concentrated, not uniform.
Despite this surface disagreement, replicates converged on the same qualitative conclusion
in 75--100\% of cases on two abstract questions, even while disagreeing widely on which
number to report. Loud disagreement about what to report can coexist with hidden consensus
about what is true. We also test whether having agents verify their own analysis, via
repeated cross-validation or bootstrapping, changes their reported confidence. Numeric
estimates tighten for genuinely robust effects, while instability is exposed in fragile
ones: sign agreement on a small effect fell from 90\% to 70\% once checked. Repeating the
study with a different model, Claude Haiku in place of Sonnet, replicates the overall
pattern. It also surfaces a new failure mode: on one question, both models agreed almost
completely on what to measure, yet reached opposite conclusions about the value itself, a
14$\times$ swing traced to an invisible preprocessing choice. Finally, we independently
re-executed every replicate's saved code against the original data to check our own
numbers: 208/240 (87\%) reproduced within tolerance. Sonnet had zero contradictions between
reported and re-executed values. Haiku had four: cases where the agent's self-reported
number did not match what its own
deterministic code actually computed, a distinct reliability gap we disclose in full.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} autonomous research, measure-choice diversity, nonstandard
errors, LLM agents, self-verification

\section{Autonomous research result}
This part is for the research itself: the hypothesis, method, evidence, and primary
result generated or developed by the agent.

\subsection{Hypothesis}
\label{sec:hypothesis}

When independent human research teams are given the same dataset and question, they
routinely reach different quantitative conclusions, not from error, but because the
space of defensible analytical choices (model, controls, metric, validation scheme) is
large \citep{steegen2016,simonsohn2020,gelmanloken2013}, and different teams occupy different corners of
it, rather than one team deliberately mapping the space itself.
\citet{menkveld2024} documented
this directly: 164 independent teams analyzing the same financial dataset produced
estimates whose disagreement (``nonstandard errors'') often rivaled or exceeded each
team's own reported statistical uncertainty. \citet{gaoxiao2026} recently showed autonomous
AI agents \citep{lu2024} reproduce this: 150 independent Claude Code agents given the same six
market microstructure questions diverged sharply, driven by stable methodological forks,
with AI peer review largely failing to resolve the disagreement while exemplar exposure
did. Concurrently, \citet{bertran2026} showed this AI-agent dispersion scales cheaply
across observational social-science datasets, using a separate AI auditor to screen each
run for validity.

All three studies are confined to observational, finance, or social-science-style
empirical analysis, where the ``correct'' measure is inherently a matter of judgment
rather than something executable and checkable, unlike machine learning research, whose
central operations (train a model, evaluate it, compute a metric) are directly checkable.
None manipulates question specificity or tests same-agent self-verification (Gao and Xiao
rely on exemplar exposure; Bertran et al.\ on a separate auditor), raising two questions
none address: does nonstandard-errors disagreement appear when the domain offers mechanical
ground truth, and, since ML tasks are executable, does asking agents to verify their own
findings via a stability check reduce disagreement the way exemplar exposure did for Gao
and Xiao, given that this kind of self-check has no analogue where the underlying
``truth'' cannot itself be re-executed?

We hypothesize that (H-a) measure-choice diversity replicates in the ML domain, driven by
whether a question's operationalization is specified, and (H-b) self-verification changes
reported numeric confidence, tightening robust effects and exposing instability in fragile
ones, without reopening the measure choice, since verification runs after that choice is
made. H-a predicts higher diversity for abstract than concrete questions; H-b predicts the
verification arm shifts numeric dispersion/sign agreement depending on the underlying
effect's robustness.

\subsection{Method}

\textbf{Dataset.} All replicates analyze a single, static copy of the UCI/OpenML Adult
(Census Income) dataset \citep{beckerkohavi1996} (48{,}842 rows, mixed categorical/numeric
features, binary income target, ${\sim}$24\% positive class), fetched once and frozen so
every replicate receives byte-identical data.

\textbf{Research questions.} We wrote six research questions about this dataset: two
abstract/concrete pairs, plus two abstract questions with an implicit method, testing
whether method convergence can coexist with target divergence
(Table~\ref{tab:hypotheses}; Section~\ref{sec:evidence}). Each replicate agent
receives only the dataset and one research question: no documentation, no access to any
other replicate's work, and no information about which other measures might be
reasonable.

\textbf{Isolation and scale.} Every replicate runs as an independent, non-interactive
Claude Code CLI invocation (\texttt{claude -p ... --dangerously-skip-permissions}) in a
freshly created, unpredictably-named temporary directory, with an explicit instruction not
to explore outside its own working directory; up to 8 replicates run concurrently, so
sibling directories do coexist (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). We collected 10
independent replicates per (hypothesis
$\times$ arm) cell: 120 replicates total with Claude Sonnet, plus a matching 120-replicate
cross-model check with Claude Haiku (Section~\ref{sec:research-loop}).

\textbf{The verification arm.} Half of the replicates for each hypothesis (the ``verify''
arm) receive an additional instruction: before reporting, validate your finding's
stability via repeated cross-validation with different seeds, a bootstrap confidence
interval, or a held-out re-test split, and report whether it held up. The other half
(``no-verify'') report directly after a single analysis pass.

\textbf{Structured reporting.} Every replicate writes a structured result (a named metric,
its value, a direction, and free-text on its methodological choices) rather than a forced
common statistical form, since abstract questions admit incompatible units.

\textbf{Verification of our own data.} Independent of the verification \emph{arm}, we
re-executed every replicate's saved code against the original data: 208/240 (87\%) reproduced.
Sonnet showed zero contradicting values; 4/120 Haiku replicates did, traced to
self-reports not matching their own deterministic code (Section~\ref{sec:verification}).

\subsection{Evidence}
\label{sec:evidence}

\textbf{Measure-choice diversity is concentrated, not uniform.} Figure~\ref{fig:diversity}
shows the number of distinct measure framings each hypothesis produced across 20
replicates, collapsing pure wording differences (fold counts, ``test set'' mentions) into
one canonical form (coding rule in Appendix~\ref{sec:reproducibility}). The picture is
uneven: H1 (abstract) produced 10 framings, entirely from disagreement over \emph{which
pair} of models to compare -- the underlying metric (ROC-AUC) is uniform. H4 (abstract)
produced 3, genuinely different base metrics (balanced accuracy, macro-F1, ROC-AUC). H2
(concrete) produced 1; H3 and H6 -- both abstract, both with an implicit method -- also
converge to 1: every H3 replicate uses permutation-importance ROC-AUC drop, every H6
replicate uses 10-bin ECE \citep{guo2017}, differing only in which feature or model is
named. Pooled per
arm of 10, abstract questions still average more distinct measures than concrete (2.6 vs.\
1.3, 2.1$\times$) with a less dominant modal framing (73\% vs.\ 98\%, 1.3$\times$), driven
almost entirely by H1 and H4.

\textbf{Surface disagreement overstates substantive disagreement.} H1's diversity count
alone would suggest near-total disagreement, but that is too strong a conclusion: 20/20 replicates
(100\%) agreed a tree-ensemble model (Gradient Boosting \citep{friedman2001},
HistGradientBoosting, or Random Forest \citep{breiman2001}) outranks linear/naive
baselines, despite no majority consensus on which pair to
headline (10/20 unique framings). For H4, 15/20 (75\%) explicitly stated the full pattern
``balanced accuracy/minority recall improves, ROC-AUC stays roughly flat''; 18/20 (90\%)
agreed at minimum on ``improves,'' despite 3 distinct base metrics.

\textbf{Verification changes numeric confidence, not measure choice.}
Figure~\ref{fig:verifyarm} compares the no-verify and verify arms for H2 and H5. For H2
(a robust, consistently-signed effect), sign agreement is 100\% in both arms, and verification tightens
the spread (SD 0.0038 $\rightarrow$ 0.0022). For H5 (small and fragile), sign
agreement \emph{falls} under verification, 90\% $\rightarrow$ 70\%, and the mean effect
shrinks toward zero ($-0.0028 \rightarrow -0.0016$): several verify-arm replicates that saw
an effect on one split found, once checked via repeated CV, that the sign was unstable
across folds, and reported this explicitly. This is unlikely to be pure split-to-split
randomness: all 20 replicates set \texttt{random\_state=42} for their primary split, though
a shared seed does not guarantee identical folds once split size, CV scheme, and
preprocessing also vary; the likelier source is differing pipeline choices (imputation,
encoding, SMOTE parameters), the same invisible-fork pattern as the H2 reversal below.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{fig2_verify_arm.pdf}
  \caption{Replicate-level values for H2 and H5 by arm. Verification tightens a robust
  effect (H2) and shrinks a fragile one toward zero (H5); black bars show arm means.}
  \label{fig:verifyarm}
\end{figure}

\textbf{A cross-model check surfaces hidden disagreement despite apparent
consensus.} Core patterns replicate with Haiku: framing diversity is concentrated in the
same two hypotheses (Table~\ref{tab:crossmodel}), and H1's substantive convergence holds
(18/20 Haiku vs.\ 20/20 Sonnet agree a tree ensemble wins). H3 is the clearest case where
Haiku's extra diversity is a real methodological difference rather than wording (4 vs.\ 1):
Sonnet always uses permutation importance, while Haiku splits across permutation,
impurity-based (Gini), and multi-method
consensus scores -- a real difference in \emph{method}, not just wording. More strikingly,
H2 (where both models agree completely on \emph{what} to measure, ROC-AUC difference
RF$-$LogReg) reverses on \emph{what value they get}: Sonnet unanimously finds logistic
regression beats random forest (mean $-0.0048$, both arms); 85\% of Haiku replicates find
the opposite, 14$\times$ larger in magnitude (mean $+0.0675$), traced to an unprompted
encoding difference the brief never specifies. Reading the saved code directly (self-report
undercounts this): Sonnet one-hot encodes in 108/120 replicates across all hypotheses
(12/120 use ordinal encoding only), Haiku uses \texttt{LabelEncoder} in 118/120, invisible
in the measure name. H5, the other question
where both models converge on a single measure, does \emph{not} show this pattern: all 40
replicates across both models and arms report the SMOTE effect as below the question's own
0.02 threshold, so this mirror-image failure mode is currently evidenced by one question,
not two, and should not be over-generalized.

\subsection{Primary Result}

Across six research questions and 240 replicates (120 Sonnet, 120 Haiku): (1) question
specificity can drive large disagreement, extending nonstandard errors in AI-conducted
research \citep{gaoxiao2026,menkveld2024} to ML, concentrated in specific questions rather
than uniform across abstract wording; (2) surface disagreement overstates genuine
disagreement: conclusions converge in
75--100\% of cases despite near-total disagreement on what to report; (3) self-verification
recalibrates confidence without touching measure choice, tightening robust effects and
surfacing fragile ones; (4) core patterns replicate across a second, differently-sized
Claude model, but agreement on \emph{what} to measure does not guarantee the value: for H2,
the second model reverses a unanimous finding.

\subsection{Agent and Harness}

The primary experimental replicates were produced by Claude Sonnet \citep{anthropic2026},
invoked through the standalone Claude Code CLI (\texttt{@anthropic-ai/claude-code},
v2.1.221) rather than through the Anthropic API directly, using the author's existing
subscription rather than separate metered billing. Each replicate is a single,
non-interactive invocation:
\texttt{claude -p "<brief>" --dangerously-skip-permissions --model sonnet}, run with its
working directory set to a freshly created, unpredictably-named temporary directory
containing only a static copy of the dataset. \texttt{--dangerously-skip-permissions} was
necessary for unattended batch execution across well over a hundred replicate runs, a real
reduction in interactive safety guardrails relative to normal Claude Code use. The
cross-model check (Section~\ref{sec:evidence}) used an identical harness and brief
template, invoked with \texttt{--model haiku} in place of \texttt{--model sonnet}.

Isolation between replicates was approximated at two levels: a fresh, non-shared
filesystem location per replicate, and an explicit brief instruction not to read, list, or
explore any directory other than the replicate's own working directory. This is weaker
than the container/Singularity-based isolation used in the most directly comparable prior
work \citep{gaoxiao2026}, which reports having observed agents reading sibling outputs
without such isolation during their own trial runs; we did not build container-level
isolation for this laptop-scale study (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

Every replicate was asked to produce a structured result file (a named primary metric, its
value, a qualitative direction, and free-text methodological-choices text) plus its
analysis code as a saved script, avoiding the unit-normalization problem
\citet{gaoxiao2026} had to solve after data collection by asking each agent to self-report
one named metric and its own reasoning rather than forcing a common statistical form up
front.

\subsection{Tools}

The agent's own toolset within each replicate session was the standard Claude Code toolset
(file read/write, bash execution) with no additional MCP servers or external tools
configured; agents wrote and executed their own Python analysis code, predominantly using
scikit-learn \citep{pedregosa2011} (240/240), pandas \citep{mckinney2010} (240/240), and
numpy \citep{harris2020} (239/240). For the class-imbalance hypotheses, 78 of 80 H4/H5
replicates used imbalanced-learn \citep{lemaitre2017} to apply SMOTE \citep{chawla2002}.

Table~\ref{tab:models} breaks down the model classes agents chose across all 240
replicates. \texttt{RandomForestClassifier} alone appears in 220/240 (92\%), far ahead of
\texttt{LogisticRegression} (180) and everything else. Agents converge on which tool to use
even while diverging on how to report what it found.

\subsection{Research Loop}
\label{sec:research-loop}

The research loop had three phases: topic selection, data collection, and analysis.

\textbf{Topic selection} involved an extended, iterative novelty-checking process: roughly
fifteen candidate research questions across three genres (LLM agent behavior/reliability;
classical ML mechanisms; large-scale systematic ML audits) were proposed and checked
against current literature via web search, and the great majority were found to already
have close prior art, in several cases published within weeks of the check. The direction
pursued here followed only after an earlier framing was found to substantially overlap
with \citet{gaoxiao2026} and adjacent recent work, prompting a search for a structurally
distinct angle: the ML domain those studies could not reach.

\textbf{Data collection} proceeded in five retry waves (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}) rather
than a single pass, since the harness resumed only cells without a completed replicate
whenever the Claude Code CLI's session usage limit was hit. Collection spanned roughly 33
calendar hours, though only about 3--4 hours involved any replicate actively running (the
rest was idle time reflecting both the session limit and when the human author was next
available to relaunch collection), and the practical per-window ceiling (roughly 15--56
successful heavy-agentic runs) proved far smaller and more variable than anticipated.
Collection also surfaced a genuine failure mode, disproportionate among compute-heavy
verification-arm replicates: agents would spawn their analysis as a background shell task
and end their turn awaiting a completion notification that could never arrive in a
single-shot, non-interactive session. An explicit brief instruction against this reduced
but did not eliminate the pattern. A separate lifecycle issue (three dispatch slots
each succeeding twice under different run identifiers, traced to an orchestration process
halted without terminating its already-spawned subprocesses) was resolved by keeping the
earlier-started run of each pair, a rule fixed before any outcome was inspected. The
dataset was finally trimmed to a uniform N=10 per cell (120 total) via a fixed
replicate-index range set at dispatch time.

\textbf{Analysis} proceeded through iterative drafting, figure generation, and review,

\subsection{Human Interventions}
\label{sec:human-interventions}

The human author made every scope decision throughout the loop described in
Section~\ref{sec:research-loop}: rejecting an initial research direction once it was found
to overlap with recent prior art and directing the agent toward the ML-domain extension;
choosing the execution mechanism (CLI over a raw API key) and model-family scope (single
family, deferred rather than abandoned); setting and defending the replicate count (N=10
per cell) against a further reduction to N=5 the human rejected on statistical grounds,
since the verification-arm comparison (this paper's most novel manipulation) would have
had too little power at that size; and reviewing drafted figures and text at each stage.
During review, the human author caught a framing problem the agent had not flagged: an
early draft title reused a precursor paper's exact title as a substring, reading as a
derivative reskin before a reader reached the abstract; it was changed to lead with this
paper's own contribution instead. The human author also set the
boundary that all reported figures (including replicate counts and the record of process
incidents in this section) must reflect what actually happened, rather than a
retrospectively cleaner narrative, and that this section's substantial drafting
collaboration with the agent be disclosed rather than presented as solely human-authored,
consistent with, and enforcing, this workshop's disclosure policy.

\subsection{Verification}
\label{sec:verification}

Two distinct verification mechanisms appear in this paper and should not be conflated. The
\textbf{verification arm} is an experimental manipulation under study
(Section~\ref{sec:evidence}), not a
claim about data quality. Separately, we independently verified the \textbf{correctness of
the underlying dataset} by re-executing every replicate's saved analysis code against the
original data and comparing the reproduced primary metric to the one
originally reported, a check made possible, and cheap, by the fact that ML analyses are
directly executable, unlike the empirical-finance analyses in the most comparable prior
work.

For the primary Sonnet dataset, 113 of 120 replicates (94\%) reproduced exactly or within
small numerical tolerance, and zero replicates showed a script running to completion and
producing a value that contradicted its own report; every discrepancy traced to a
limitation of the re-verification method itself: 6 replicates' saved scripts were not
fully self-contained (the majority cause) and 1 timed out on the heaviest verification-arm
script.

Running the same check on the Haiku dataset surfaced a different and more concerning
pattern: 95 of 120 replicates (79\%) reproduced, and this time 4 replicates' saved scripts
ran to completion and produced a value that contradicted their own report. We manually
re-ran a sample of these mismatching scripts twice each and confirmed they are fully
deterministic (identical output both times), ruling out simple run-to-run non-determinism;
the discrepancy is between what the saved code computes and what was self-reported,
apparently produced independently rather than derived from that code's own execution. The
remaining 21
non-reproducing Haiku replicates match the same not-self-contained-script pattern seen in
Sonnet, at a notably higher rate (17.5\% vs.\ 5\%). Table~\ref{tab:verification}
summarizes both datasets.

We disclose all of this plainly: re-execution establishes that reported numbers were not
fabricated on the runs it could fully check and confirm, but it also surfaced (rather
than merely failed to rule out) a real, model-specific self-report reliability
gap. This is itself consistent with this paper's main finding: verification is not one
thing, and a check aimed at one failure mode (fabrication) can, run rigorously enough,
catch a different one (self-report/execution inconsistency) it was not specifically
designed to find.

\subsection{Significance of result}

The result matters for two audiences. For researchers using AI agents as empirical
collaborators, it is a direct warning: an underspecified request fails quietly, not
loudly, returning a confident answer to a subtly different question, and the fix
(specifying the measure, not just the goal) is cheap and available today. For the autonomous-research community this workshop represents, the more
important result may be that a verification step targeting numerical stability structurally
cannot resolve disagreement about what to measure. As autonomous agents gain more latitude
in real research pipelines, ``have the agent check its own work'' is a natural first line
of defense; this result suggests that defense has a specific blind spot, closable only by a
different intervention (in the spirit of the exemplar-exposure result of
\citet{gaoxiao2026}), not a more thorough version of the same one. One caveat:
Section~\ref{sec:hypothesis} compares self-verification to exemplar exposure, but they differ: exemplar
exposure could change which measure agents pick, while our stability check runs only after
that choice is made.

\subsection{Interpretability}

All experimental artifacts were tracked to support post-hoc audit: every replicate's raw
result, code, and metadata went to a dedicated per-run directory, and every batch dispatch
and retry wave was logged, one line per attempt, so the full history is independently
reconstructable rather than overwritten. Excluded and superseded runs were archived, not
deleted. A separate chronological process log
recording every scope decision and incident is available as supplementary material;
Sections~\ref{sec:research-loop}--\ref{sec:human-interventions} are drawn directly from it.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Isolation is prompt-level and filesystem-based, not OS-level sandboxing; up to 8
replicates run concurrently so siblings coexist, and we cannot rule out imperfect
isolation, though found no evidence of cross-contamination. This study covers two Claude
models of different capability tiers (Sonnet, Haiku), not distinct families; broader
coverage (e.g.\ Opus, non-Anthropic) was not attempted, and Haiku's re-verification
followed initial collection rather than running alongside it. N=10 per cell, well below
the 150 in the most comparable prior study, was sized for laptop/subscription-quota
feasibility, not power analysis. Our re-execution tolerance ($\pm$1\% absolute or 5\%
relative) exceeds the entire effect size for H2 and H5, so a small-effect replicate can
pass despite a materially different re-executed value; under a stricter same-file
criterion, reproduction is 108/120 (90\%) for Sonnet and 94/120 (78\%) for Haiku, versus
the reported 113/120 and 95/120. Measure-choice diversity and H4's substantive-agreement
figures use a documented but judgment-based coding rule
(Appendix~\ref{sec:reproducibility}); a different reasonable coding could shift counts by
one or two.

\section{Reflections and Broader Impact}

This paper's main practical implication is a warning with an inverted risk profile from
the usual failure mode people worry about with autonomous agents: the danger here is not
that an agent visibly fails, but that it succeeds confidently and quietly at a slightly
different task than intended, in a way that is only detectable by comparing many
independent attempts. Our own Sonnet-vs-Haiku encoding difference
(Section~\ref{sec:evidence}) is a clean
illustration: 108/120 Sonnet replicates one-hot encoded categorical features (reading the
saved code, not self-report), 118/120 Haiku replicates used label encoding instead, and
no replicate's self-reported metric name hinted at the difference. As autonomous
agents take on a larger share of applied ML work (model selection, feature engineering,
evaluation design), this failure mode will mostly go unnoticed by a single researcher
running a single analysis, since nothing about a lone run signals that a different, equally
defensible choice existed. The positive implication is equally direct: this risk is cheap
to mitigate where it actually bites. H1 (10 distinct framings) and its
concretely-specified counterpart H2 (1 framing) are the same underlying comparison; naming
the model pair, not just the metric, is what separates them, at zero additional cost. The
same intervention would likely help H4 (which metric family), but does little for H3, H5,
or H6, which converge on their own. We would encourage practitioners delegating open-ended
ML analysis to agents to specify both the metric and the comparison target explicitly,
rather than assuming the metric alone is enough.

For this workshop's broader question of how the field should adapt, we would highlight
three points beyond this paper's specific result. First, our own novelty-checking process
(roughly fifteen candidate directions checked against current literature, the great
majority already covered by work published within weeks) suggests that ``we searched
and found nothing'' is weakening as evidence of novelty specifically for autonomous
research submissions, since the same tools that accelerate one group's research loop
accelerate everyone else's. Venues may need to treat rapid, independent convergent
discovery as an expected feature of the genre rather than a sign that a submission was
insufficiently diligent. Second, self-verification is not one thing: this paper required
two structurally different verification steps (an experimental manipulation testing
whether agents checking their own numerical stability changes what they report, and an
independent check that reported numbers were not fabricated), and conflating them would
have been easy to do by accident. As ``have the agent check its own work'' becomes a
standard mitigation for autonomous-agent unreliability, we would encourage reviewers and
discussants to ask specifically which kind of wrongness a given verification step could
and could not have caught, rather than treating any plausible-sounding check as covering
all of them. Third, a laptop and a subscription are not yet a research lab, and the gap is
measurable: this paper's Sonnet collection alone needed roughly 33 calendar hours and five
retry waves despite only 3--4 hours of active computation (Section~\ref{sec:research-loop}),
a limit imposed by an artificial usage cap, not by compute capability, and compounded by
tooling reliability: Sonnet succeeded on only 37\% of dispatched attempts per try,
consistent with, though not conclusively caused by, the background-task
completion-notification bug described above (we did not log which cause ended each failed
attempt). The most comparable prior study ran 150 agents against metered billing with no
such wall \citep{gaoxiao2026}. Access to a research lab increasingly means access to a
\emph{budget} and to tooling mature enough to finish what it starts. That shapes which
questions are practically askable, not just how quickly they get answered, and is worth
this workshop's attention, since it bears on who gets to participate in autonomous
research at all.

This point held during our own review process, not just in the collected data: the Haiku
re-verification (Section~\ref{sec:verification}) was nearly skipped as redundant, since
re-running a pipeline already applied to Sonnet seemed unlikely to surface anything new. It
caught the paper's most significant integrity finding instead, a genuine self-report/execution
mismatch absent from Sonnet entirely. A check that already ``passed once'' feeling safe to
skip on a second dataset is exactly the instinct worth resisting; that is precisely where a
verification step earns its cost.

We do not see meaningful negative societal impact specific to this work: it uses a
standard public benchmark dataset, introduces no new model or capability, and its
practical recommendation (specify measures explicitly) reduces rather than increases risk
from autonomous-agent-driven research.

\section{Reproducibility Statement}
\label{sec:reproducibility}

Three early pilot replicates (used only to validate the harness mechanics before full
collection began) are excluded from every reported figure and archived separately from the
240 replicates the paper analyzes.

\textbf{Measure-choice diversity coding rule.} A replicate's metric name is reduced to its
base statistical measure (e.g.\ ROC-AUC, balanced accuracy, macro-F1, minority-class F1,
Expected Calibration Error, or a feature-importance sub-type: permutation, impurity/Gini,
or multi-method consensus), stripping wording that does not change what is computed
(fold/CV/test-set phrasing, which specific feature or model is named). For H1 only, which
pair of model families is compared is kept as part of the code, since the question ``does
model family affect performance'' is not well-defined without choosing a pair; for H3's
``top feature'' and H6's ``which model to calibrate,'' the named entity is an output of a
fixed method rather than a chosen operationalization, so it is not counted (model choice is
reported separately, Table~\ref{tab:models}/\ref{tab:toolvsmethod}). This is a documented, but ultimately judgment-based,
coding decision; the exact rule is implemented in \texttt{harness/analyze.py}
(\texttt{base\_metric} and \texttt{h1\_comparison\_target}) and is re-run, not hand-edited,
to produce every diversity number in this paper. The H4 substantive-agreement figures
(Section~\ref{sec:evidence}) use a similarly documented rule: a replicate counts toward the
full ``improves, ROC-AUC flat'' pattern only if it explicitly states both clauses, and
toward ``improves'' alone if it states any accuracy/recall-family improvement, even without
an explicit ROC-AUC clause.

The dataset (UCI/OpenML Adult/Census Income \citep{beckerkohavi1996}) is public and freely
available; no dataset-level documentation in the style of \citet{gebru2021} accompanies it
beyond the UCI repository page, which is itself disclosed to replicate agents as the only
provenance information they receive. The six research questions are given in full in
Table~\ref{tab:hypotheses}; the exact brief text given to each replicate agent is available
as supplementary material
(\texttt{harness/hypotheses.py}) and has five parts: (1) role framing as an independent,
single-shot researcher with no access to other replicates' work; (2) the dataset and its
row count, with no further documentation; (3) the one research question this replicate
receives, plus an explicit instruction to make its own methodological choices (named
examples: preprocessing, \emph{encoding}, split, hyperparameters, metric) for anything the
question leaves unspecified; (4) an explicit instruction to run everything synchronously in
the foreground rather than backgrounding long-running commands, since no follow-up turn
will arrive (Section~\ref{sec:research-loop}); and (5) a required \texttt{result.json}
schema (summary, named metric, value, direction, methodological choices), extended with
two verification
fields for the verify arm. Reproducing the replicate-collection process requires the Claude
Code CLI and access to Claude Sonnet and Claude Haiku under the same or comparable
versions; because model
behavior can drift across versions, we do not expect bit-exact reproduction of individual
replicate outputs, but do expect the qualitative pattern (concentrated, question-specific
measure-choice diversity rather than a uniform abstract-vs-concrete split; verification
tightening robust effects and surfacing fragile ones) to be checkable by any lab with CLI
access to a comparable agent. All analysis code
(harness, verification, figure generation) is standard Python/scikit-learn and is included
in supplementary material along with the full experimental log described in
Section~\ref{sec:research-loop} and a fuller set of representative replicate examples than
Appendix~\ref{sec:examples} shows. \emph{[Author note: confirm final code/data hosting
location (e.g., an anonymized repository) before submission, and update this
statement with the access details.]}
